# Embeddings explained

An embedding is a list of numbers, a vector, that represents a piece of text. An embedding model reads the text and outputs a vector with a fixed number of dimensions, for example 768, no matter how long the text was. Texts with similar meanings get vectors that point in similar directions, even when they share no words. "How do I reset my password?" and "I forgot my login" end up close together, while "Password managers store credentials" ends up further away even though it shares a word with the first. The individual dimensions have no human-readable meaning. Only a vector's position relative to other vectors carries information. The documents and the questions must be embedded with the same model, so switching embedding models means re-embedding every document.

Embedding models are trained with contrastive learning. The model sees pairs of texts that belong together, such as a question and the passage that answers it, and pairs that do not. Training adjusts the model so that matching pairs get vectors close to each other and non-matching pairs get vectors far apart. The training pairs decide what "similar" means to the model. A model trained on search queries and their answers is good at matching questions to passages, while a model trained on paraphrases is good at spotting sentences that say the same thing. Embedding models are much smaller than chat models. EmbeddingGemma, for example, has about 300 million parameters, while even small chat models have billions.

To compare two embeddings, retrieval systems use a similarity measure. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors and ignores their length. It is 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they are perpendicular and -1 when they point in opposite directions. The dot product multiplies matching dimensions and adds up the results, so it depends on both the angle and the lengths of the vectors. When every vector is normalized to length 1, the dot product and cosine similarity produce the same ranking, which is why many systems normalize embeddings once and then use the cheaper dot product. Euclidean distance measures the straight-line distance between two points, and smaller means more similar. For normalized vectors it ranks results in the same order as cosine similarity.
